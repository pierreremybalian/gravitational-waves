On 14 September 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC, the two Advanced LIGO detectors were in their final engineering run before observing began. Livingston saw a signal first; 7 milliseconds later, Hanford saw the same shape. The automatic search flagged it within three minutes. It took the collaboration five months of checks before announcing, on 11 February 2016, that it was the first direct detection of gravitational waves and the first observation of two black holes merging.

What you hear is not a recording of air. A gravitational wave changes distances, and the detectors measure that change; because the frequencies of this one happen to fall in the range of human hearing, the measurement can be played through a loudspeaker unchanged.
