General relativity predicts the whole waveform from a handful of numbers: two masses, two spins, the distance and the orientation. Fitting those numbers to the data is what turns a chirp into an astronomical measurement. The heavier the pair, the lower the pitch at which it merges; the chirp mass, the combination that sets how fast the frequency rises, is measured best of all.

The energy is hard to picture. For a few milliseconds at its peak the merger radiated more power in gravitational waves than all the stars in the observable universe shine in light, and almost none of it interacted with anything on the way here.

GW170817 settled a question GW150914 could not. Many theories that modify gravity predict that gravitational waves travel at a speed different from light; a single 1.7-second gap after a 130-million-year race ruled out most of them overnight.
